Objectives

What you will be able to do

Task A

Hoisting Fundamentals

Execute vertical load handling correctly from slack rope to suspended load.

Task A

Plumb before power

The hook goes over the load before the load comes off the ground. §1910.179(n)(3)(ii)(c) puts it as a pre-hoist condition: the hook shall be brought over the load in such a manner as to prevent swinging.

A load lifted from off-plumb does not stay where it was. It swings to plumb the instant it breaks free, and it arrives at the plumb point with speed. The further off you started, the harder it arrives.
Task A

Slack, then load, then prove the brake

Take the slack out slowly. Apply load gradually. Then stop a few inches up and prove the brake before you commit. §1910.179(n)(3)(vii): the operator shall test the brakes each time a load approaching the rated load is handled, by raising the load a few inches and applying the brakes.

There shall be no downward drift during that stop. This is the same pause that OHC-06 uses to check balance — one stop, three questions: does it hold, does it hang level, is the rigging taking load as expected.
Task A

Side pulls: not banned, but gated

§1910.179(n)(3)(iv) does not flatly prohibit side pulls. It permits them “except when specifically authorized by a responsible person who has determined that the stability of the crane is not thereby endangered and that various parts of the crane will not be overstressed.”

Two determinations, one named role, before the pick. What is prohibited is the unauthorised side pull — which is every side pull an operator decides on alone, at the controls, because the load is nearly under the hook.
Task A

What a side pull actually damages

The rope leaves the sheave groove and can jump the sheave entirely, cutting itself on the flange. Drum spooling goes irregular, so the next layer beds badly. The trolley takes side thrust its wheels and rails were never designed for.

And the bridge girder takes a lateral load it was not designed for — an overhead crane is engineered for vertical load and horizontal travel, not for dragging. This is why the damage shows up in the runway, the trolley and the reeving, not in the hook.
Task A

Only as high as the job needs

Travel height is a hazard setting, not a convenience. The rule is the minimum practical height that clears the path — the DOE Hanford manual states it as “never raise the load higher than necessary.”

Height buys nothing and costs everything: a longer pendulum, a wider swing footprint, more energy in a drop, and a longer time to react. §1910.179(n)(3)(vi) then adds the hard one — the employer shall require that the operator avoid carrying loads over people.
Task A

Two-blocking, and why the limit switch is not the answer

Two-blocking is contact between the load block and the upper block, trolley or drum. Keep hoisting into it and something parts — usually the rope, with the load underneath.

The upper limit switch exists to catch it. But §1910.179(n)(4)(ii) says the switch shall never be used as an operating control, and (k)(1)(ii) explains why: its trip point is set by test, with an empty hook. It was never calibrated for a loaded block at speed. Prevention is yours: know your headroom, watch the block, slow the approach.
Task A

The other end of the drum

The upper limit gets all the attention. The lower one is a number and it is absolute: §1910.179(n)(3)(viii) — the load shall not be lowered below the point where less than two full wraps of rope remain on the hoisting drum.

Those two wraps are what holds the rope's dead-end anchorage from taking the whole load. Deep pits, low floors and below-grade placements are where operators run out of drum, and a lower limit switch is not always fitted to stop them.

Task B

Travel and Drift Control

Control bridge and trolley travel and arrest load swing.

Task B

Two axes make one path

Bridge and trolley are independent motions at right angles. Run them one at a time and the load walks an L. Run them together and the resultant is a diagonal — which is usually the shorter, smoother path and the one with fewer start-stop swing events.

Diagonal travel is a skill, not a shortcut. The two motions have different speeds and different inertias, so holding a straight resultant means modulating both, not pinning both buttons and hoping.
Task B

Every swing has a cause and you supplied it

A suspended load only swings if something accelerated it. The three causes: acceleration at the start, deceleration at the stop, and plugging — reversing the direction control while still moving, to brake.

§1910.179(n)(3)(iii)(a) is the rule underneath all three: during hoisting, care shall be taken that there is no sudden acceleration or deceleration of the moving load. Plugging is the most violent of the three and the most avoidable.
Task B

Arrest the swing by following it

You cannot stop a pendulum by holding the pivot still. You stop it by moving the pivot under the load — drive the trolley or bridge in the direction the load is swinging, then ease off as the load comes back to plumb beneath the hook.

Fighting the swing feeds it. Following it kills it in one or two cycles. The technique is the same at every scale; only the timing changes with rope length.
Task B

Inching and float

Inching is momentary control taps — short pulses that move the crane a fraction and let it settle, used for the last few inches of approach. On a variable-frequency drive it is the lowest speed step; on a two-speed contactor crane it is discipline with the first notch.

Float is holding the load just clear of the surface while alignment is confirmed. Both trade speed for control, and both are the difference between placing a load and landing one.
Task B

Clearance is a duty, continuously

EM 385-1-1 16-8.aa(5) is the operating rule: clearance shall be maintained between the crane and any structure or object, any parallel running cranes, and cranes operating at different elevations.

All three, all the time. The one operators lose is the third — a crane on a different runway at a different height is outside the normal scan and is the one that surprises people.
Task B

The 3 and 2 are design numbers, not your margin

§1910.179(b)(6)(i) sets a minimum 3 in overhead and 2 in lateral clearance, in conformity with CMAA Specification No. 61, incorporated by reference through §1910.6. (b)(7) adds clearance between parallel cranes.

Read what that is: it is the clearance the installed crane structure has from the building. It is not an operating tolerance for a swinging load, and quoting it as one is a category error. Your working clearance is set by the load, the swing and the path — and it is a great deal more than two inches.
Task B

Approaching stops and other cranes

EM 385-1-1 16-8.aa(6): contacts with runway stops or other cranes shall be made with extreme caution, with particular care for persons on or below the crane, and only after making certain that persons on the other cranes are aware.

Note what it does not say. It does not prohibit contact — bumping a stop is a normal end-of-runway event. It requires that it be deliberate, slow, and known about by everyone it could affect.
Task B

Sound the warning

§1910.179(n)(3)(xi) names two triggers: when starting the bridge, and when the load or hook approaches near or over personnel. The warning signal shall be sounded.

It is a habit that decays fastest in familiar bays, where the operator knows who is where — which is precisely where the person who moved is not accounted for.

Task C

Precision Placement

Place loads at target within tolerance without contact or shock.

Task C

Take the axes off one at a time

A precision placement is not one manoeuvre, it is a controlled reduction. Bring the load close on all axes, then settle one axis at a time — stop the bridge, let it settle, correct the trolley, let it settle, then lower.

Compounding motions on final approach is how a placement becomes a collision. Every motion you still have running is a variable you are managing while the load is inches from something hard.
Task C

Set-down is a transfer, not a drop

Lower until the load takes its own weight and the rope begins to slack. Stop there. That is the transfer point, and it is where you confirm the load is seated, stable and bearing where it is meant to bear.

Watch the rope, not the load. Rope going slack unevenly means the load is seating on one corner — it will tip or slide when you continue. Take it back up and reset.
Task C

Fixtures, machines and racks

Placing into something is harder than placing onto something. Fixtures and racks add close-tolerance contact hazards on three or four sides at once, and the load blocks the operator's view of exactly the surfaces that matter.

Machines add a second problem: the thing you are placing into may be capable of moving, powered, or holding stored energy. Placement into a machine is an energy control question before it is a crane question.
Task C

Blind placements belong to the signaler

When the operator cannot see the landing point, the placement is executed on signals. The operator's judgement does not fill the gap — it substitutes a guess for information.

The discipline that makes it safe: one signaler, agreed before the lift, continuously visible. Signal lost, motion stops. Signalling is developed in OHC-09; here the point is narrower — a blind placement without a signaler is not a placement, it is a hope.
Task C

Verify transfer before slacking anything

The load is fully transferred when it is stable, supported and bearing as intended. Until all three are confirmed, the rigging stays under tension and the crane stays engaged.

§1910.179(n)(3)(x): the operator does not leave the position at the controls while the load is suspended. A load resting but not yet verified is still the crane's load.
Task C

The moment that crushes people

Set-down is where hands go in. The load is low, it looks placed, the job is nearly done, and someone reaches to guide a corner or free a sling. The load is still moving, still capable of shifting, and it weighs what it always weighed.

§1910.179(n)(3)(v) covers the extreme case: while any employee is on the load or hook, there shall be no hoisting, lowering, or traveling. The working rule is broader — hands stay out until the load is verified down.
