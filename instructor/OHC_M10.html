Task A — Facility and Layout Hazards

Manage hazards created by the building, storage, and traffic environment.

  1. Obstruction is a regulated condition, not just untidiness. §1910.179(b)(6)(ii): where passageways or walkways are provided, obstructions shall not be placed so that safety of personnel will be jeopardized by movements of the crane.Read the test in that sentence — it is not “is the aisle tidy”, it is could the crane's movement make this stack dangerous to somebody. (b)(6) is in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list, so it binds on both branches.
  2. The clearance envelope belongs to the crane, permanently. A runway has a swept volume that is the crane's at all times, whether or not the crane is in it. Material staged inside that envelope is not “in the way today”; it is inside a space that was never available.Storage creep is gradual and it is nobody's decision. A pallet goes down for an hour, a rack is extended by one bay, a delivery is staged where there was room. The envelope does not move to accommodate any of it.
  3. Traffic that is not part of the lift. Pedestrians and vehicles cross load paths, and neither is watching the ceiling. A forklift driver is looking at their forks and their mirrors; a pedestrian is looking at where they are walking.Two different controls. Pedestrians need barriers and signage that work on somebody who never attended a briefing. Vehicles need coordination with the driver or their supervisor, because a forklift cannot stop or steer like a person.
  4. What is between the crane and the ceiling. Piping, lighting, ductwork, conduit, sprinkler mains, cable tray and adjacent equipment all occupy the space between the load's travel height and the structure. Most of it is fragile, some of it is energised, and some of it is both.The load's highest point is what clears these, not the hook. A load rigged with tall lifting lugs or a spreader beam has a different profile from the same load in slings.
  5. Floors matter more for pendant operators. A pendant or floor-operated crane makes the operator a pedestrian who is walking backwards while watching a load. Housekeeping, floor openings, hoses, spills and slick surfaces stop being background conditions and become the primary trip and fall risk.The path the operator walks is part of the lift plan, not just the path the load takes. Walk it before you load it.
  6. Lighting — and what the standard actually covers. §1910.179(c)(4) is one sentence: light in the cab shall be sufficient to enable the operator to see clearly enough to perform his work. That is the whole of the lighting requirement in the standard.Note the scope. It is the cab — not the load path, not the placement point, and not floor-operated cranes at all. (c) is also not in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list, so it is facility-branch. Load-path and placement lighting is governed by Part 1910 Subpart D and the facility programme, and the ACS's broader reading of `A.K5` is the correct working rule — it just is not what (c)(4) says.
  7. The path you surveyed is not the path you fly. A path cleared at the start of a shift is a snapshot. Storage moves, staging arrives, a machine is opened for maintenance. The survey has a shelf life measured in hours, and the assumption that it does not is what puts a load into something.Re-survey when anything about the environment changed, and treat “I ran this yesterday” as information about yesterday.

Task B — Environmental Conditions

Apply environmental limits for outdoor and special-service cranes.

  1. Wind on an outdoor crane. Wind acts on two things: the load, where it becomes a horizontal force proportional to sail area, and the crane, which is a large structure on wheels on a rail.EM 385-1-1 16-8.aa(7) gives the operator duty: operators of outdoor cranes shall secure them when leaving. §1910.179(b)(4) gives the hardware for outdoor storage bridges — automatic rail clamps and a wind-indicating device with a visible or audible alarm at a predetermined wind velocity.
  2. There is no published wind number for overhead cranes — and that matters. §1910.179(b)(4) requires the alarm at a predetermined velocity. It does not say what that velocity is. The number is set by the manufacturer or the facility, and it is the number you must know for your own crane.Two numbers get imported wrongly. The DOE Hanford manual's 25 mph is a trigger for evaluation by a qualified person, not an operating limit. EM 385's 20 mph figure governs tower crane climbing — a different class, a different operation. Neither is an overhead crane's wind limit. Get your own crane's number.
  3. Cold changes the machine; heat changes the operator. In cold, brake friction materials behave differently, lubricants stiffen, wire rope loses flexibility, and steel loses toughness — the structural concern is brittleness, not strength. Condensation that froze overnight can lock a brake or bond a clamp.In heat, the crane is largely unbothered and the operator degrades: concentration, reaction time and judgement all fall before anybody notices. In a cab without air conditioning this is the dominant hazard of a hot day.
  4. Precipitation and ice do three separate things. They add weight — accumulated water, snow or ice on a load is real mass that appears in nobody's calculation. They reduce rail traction, so wheels slip and braking distances stretch. And they cut visibility for operator, signaler and spotter at once.Ice adds a fourth: it bonds things together. A load frozen to the ground becomes a side pull the moment you take up slack, and the load breaks free with no warning and full stored energy.
  5. Special service: hot metal. §1910.179(a) defines a hot metal handling crane as one used for transporting or pouring molten material, and the standard then treats it as its own class.§1910.179(g)(4)(iii): provision shall be made to prevent broken parts or molten metal falling upon the operator or from the crane. §1910.179(f)(2)(vi): each independent hoisting unit of a crane handling hot metal with power control braking shall have at least two holding brakes. Note the branch split — **(g)** is in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list, **(f)(2)** is not.
  6. Corrosive and washdown service. Corrosive atmospheres and washdown areas attack the things inspection depends on: rope interior, electrical enclosures, brake surfaces and the legibility of markings and tags.The equipment provisions are a design matter — enclosure ratings, materials, sealed bearings. The operator's interest is narrower and constant: corrosion hides internally, so a rope in corrosive service is inspected on a shorter cycle than its appearance suggests. Cross-refs `OHC.05.C.K2`.
  7. Who is watching the weather. Weather monitoring is an assigned responsibility with a named owner and a stated suspension criterion. Unassigned, it defaults to whoever notices — which in practice is nobody, because everyone is doing their job.EM 385 §16.A puts environmental considerations into the lift plan itself: wind, storms, precipitation, and power lines in the area of travel or load swing. The criterion is written down before the lift, so that suspending is a decision already made rather than an argument to be had.

Task C — Energy and Process Hazards

Control electrical and process energy exposure during operations.

  1. Runway conductors are live and they are at working height. §1910.179(g)(6): open-type conductors mounted on runway beams or overhead shall be so located or so guarded that persons entering or leaving the cab or crane footwalk normally could not come into contact with them.Read “normally”. The guarding is designed against ordinary access, not against someone reaching, leaning or working in an unusual position. Any elevated access near a runway is an electrical job before it is a climbing job. (g) is in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list — both branches.
  2. The other conductor guard, on the crane itself. §1910.179(e)(5)(ii): a guard shall be provided to prevent contact between bridge conductors and hoisting ropes if they could come into contact.That guard is a component whose absence or damage is an inspection finding, and it is protecting against a rope energising. (e)(5) is also in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list. Cross-refs `OHC.02` and `OHC.05.B.K1`.
  3. Facility electrical along the path. Bus ducts, panels, disconnects, transformers and open equipment cabinets sit at load-path height throughout a working building. A load does not have to touch them — a sling, a tag line or a swinging corner will do.The clearance you plan is not to the equipment; it is to the swept volume of the load plus its rigging plus its swing. That is a much larger shape than the load.
  4. Process energy is quieter than electrical. Steam lines, hydraulic runs, pneumatic mains and pressurised process piping are unmarked as often as not, look like structure, and fail with stored energy rather than a flash.A struck steam line is a burn hazard over a wide area with no visible source. A struck hydraulic line at pressure injects. Neither announces itself the way a conductor does, which is why they are identified in the survey rather than recognised on the way past.
  5. Placing into equipment that is still in service. Placement into a machine, fixture or line that has not been isolated is the highest consequence item in this module. The receiving equipment can move, cycle, energise, or hold pressure while a load is being introduced into it and people are working at the interface.The rule is simple and it is a hold, not a judgement: no placement into in-service equipment. The crane waits for isolation. This is the placement where the operator's stop-work authority is most likely to be needed and most likely to be unpopular.
  6. The operator's interface with energy control. The operator is not the authorised employee under the facility's energy control programme, and does not apply or remove isolations. The interface is narrower: know that isolation is required, know who verifies it, and hold the placement until it is verified.§1910.147(c)(1) (held) requires the employer to establish a programme of energy control procedures, employee training and periodic inspections so that before servicing or maintenance the machine is isolated and rendered inoperative. The operator is typically an affected employee (definition at (b)). What the operator owes the programme is the hold.
  7. The crane's own disconnects, and where they are. §1910.179(g)(5)(i): the power supply to the runway conductors shall be controlled by a switch or circuit breaker located on a fixed structure, accessible from the floor, and arranged to be locked in the open position.And on the crane: (g)(5)(ii) cab-operated cranes get an enclosed lockable switch in the leads from the runway conductors, openable within easy reach of the operator. (g)(5)(iii) floor-operated cranes get one mounted on the bridge or footwalk near the runway collectors, plus one of three floor-reachable means: a nonconductive rope to the main disconnect, an undervoltage trip operated by the pendant emergency stop, or a main line contactor operated from the pendant.

Teach it correctly — do not guess

  • ⭐ B.K1b — three wind numbers are in circulation and none of them is your limit. §1910.179(b)(4) requires the wind alarm at a predetermined wind velocity and does not set one. The number belongs to the manufacturer or the facility. Two figures get imported by mistake: the DOE Hanford manual's 25 mph, which is a trigger for evaluation by a qualified person rather than an operating limit; and EM 385's 20 mph, which governs tower crane climbing — a different equipment class and a different operation. The ACS asks for securing thresholds without saying where the threshold comes from. B.K1b gates the correct answer: get your own crane's number.
  • ⚠️ A.K5 — the ACS reads lighting far wider than the standard does. The ACS asks for lighting adequacy for load path and placement visibility. §1910.179(c)(4) is one sentence and covers light in the cab only. It says nothing about the load path, nothing about the placement point, and nothing about floor-operated cranes — and (c) is not in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list, so it is facility-branch. The ACS's broader reading is the correct working rule; it is just not what (c)(4) says. A.K5 gates the paragraph as written and A.K5b gates where the wider duty actually comes from: Part 1910 Subpart D and the facility programme.
  • ✅ Hot metal is a defined class with two provisions on opposite branches. §1910.179(a) defines a hot metal handling crane as one used for transporting or pouring molten material. §1910.179(g)(4)(iii) requires provision to prevent broken parts or molten metal falling upon the operator or from the crane — (g) is in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list, so both branches. §1910.179(f)(2)(vi) requires at least two holding brakes on each independent hoisting unit with power control braking — (f)(2) is not in the list, so facility only. B.K4b gates the split, which is the kind of distinction that decides an audit finding.
  • ✅ Two conductor provisions, both on both branches. §1910.179(g)(6): open-type runway conductors shall be located or guarded so that persons entering or leaving the cab or footwalk normally could not come into contact with them — C.K1 gates the word normally, because the guarding is designed against ordinary access and not against reaching or working in an unusual position. §1910.179(e)(5)(ii): a guard shall prevent contact between bridge conductors and hoisting ropes — C.K1b. Both (e)(5) and (g) are in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list.
  • ✅ C.K5b — the crane's disconnects are specified in detail. §1910.179(g)(5) is more prescriptive than the ACS suggests. (g)(5)(i): runway conductor supply controlled by a switch or breaker on a fixed structure, accessible from the floor, lockable in the open position. (g)(5)(ii): cab-operated cranes get an enclosed lockable switch in the leads from the runway conductors, openable within easy reach of the operator. (g)(5)(iii): floor-operated cranes get one on the bridge or footwalk near the runway collectors, plus one of three named means — a nonconductive rope to the main disconnect, an undervoltage trip operated by the pendant emergency stop, or a main line contactor operated from the pendant.
  • 🔧 Correction carried back to OHC-06. OHC.06.B.K4 was sourced to “EM 385 §16 / ASME scope for this class” — a vague attribution. The actual anchor is §1910.179(g)(5)(v): all cranes using a lifting magnet shall have a magnet circuit switch of the enclosed type with provision for locking in the open position, and means for discharging the inductive load of the magnet shall be provided. OHC-06's trace source is corrected and the module rebuilt.
  • ⚠️ Subpart D walking-working surfaces remain unheld. A.K4 and A.K5b route to Part 1910 Subpart D (walking-working surfaces, fixed ladders — reached from §1910.179(c)(2)). Subpart D paragraph text is not held in this repo. The items stay gated at operator level (walk the path; know cab lighting is (c)(4) only) and marked OPEN. §1910.147 is now held — C.K5 gates affected vs authorized and the hold until isolation is verified, from (b) and (c)(1) fetched 2026-08-28 from OSHA.gov.
