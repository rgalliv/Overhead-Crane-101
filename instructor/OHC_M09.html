What they should be able to do after the presentation

  • Use and respond to the standard overhead crane hand signal set, and know when a signal person is required and who is permitted to be one.
  • Conduct lifts on closed-loop verbal and radio direction, with motion vocabulary anchored to fixed references and a stop-and-hold convention on communication loss.
  • Integrate spotters, riggers and adjacent trades into the lift communication plan, and honour stop-work authority from anyone.

Task A — Standard Hand Signals

Use and respond to standard overhead crane hand signals.

  1. One standard set, and it is not yours to invent. The overhead crane signal set is small and fixed: hoist, lower, bridge travel, trolley travel, stop, and emergency stop. EM 385-1-1 §16.G.05.a requires a standard signal system on all lifting and hoisting equipment — by hand, voice, audible or comparable signals.Where hand signals are used, the Standard Method must be used. The DOE Hanford manual states the same rule from the other side: the standard overhead crane and hoist hand signals adopted by ANSI standards are to be used.
  2. The hundred-foot boundary. §16.G.05.a(1): manual hand signals may be used when the distance between the operator and signal person is not more than 100 ft (30.4 m). Beyond that, hands stop working as a channel.§16.G.05.a(2): radio, telephone, or a visual and audible electrically-operated system shall be used when the distance is more than 100 ft, or when the operator and signal person cannot see each other. Either trigger, not both.
  3. Three situations that require a signal person. §16.G.05.b names them and they are not judgement calls. A signal person must be used: when the point of operation, load travel, or the area near or at load placement is not in full view of the operator; when the equipment is traveling and the view in the direction of travel is obstructed.And the third, which is the one that protects everybody: due to site-specific safety concerns, either the operator or the person handling the load determines that it is necessary. Either party can call for a signaler. Neither needs permission.
  4. Who is permitted to signal. A signal person is a qualified role, not a volunteer. §16.B.06 requires qualification by a third-party Qualified Evaluator or the employer's Qualified Evaluator, with documentation specifying each type of signaling — hand, radio, and so on — the person is qualified for.The qualification covers knowing the Standard Method, competence in applying it, and a basic understanding of crane operation and limitations, including the dynamics of swinging and stopping loads, demonstrated by written and practical test. And one clause people miss: an employer's assessment is not portable — another employer may not rely on it.
  5. One signaler at a time. §16.G.05.d, first half: only one person gives signals to the operator at a time. Two people signalling is not redundancy; it is two instructions and one crane.The signaler is agreed before the lift and is identifiable — by position, by vest, by name. If the operator cannot tell who the signaler is, the signaler has not been established.
  6. Except the one signal anybody can give. §16.G.05.d, second half: the single-signaler rule holds unless an emergency stop signal is given, which may be given by anyone and must be obeyed by the operator.This is the only signal with no authority requirement attached. Anyone who sees something can stop the crane, and the operator's duty is to comply first and ask afterwards. A stop that turns out to be unnecessary costs a minute.
  7. Where the signaler stands. Two requirements at once: visible to the operator for the whole signalling period, and clear of the load and its fall zone. Losing either one ends the lift.These conflict on tight placements, and the resolution is never to compromise the second. If the only position with a view is inside the fall zone, the answer is a different channel — radio, or a second spotter relaying — not a signaler standing under the load.
  8. Charts, and what to do when the standard does not fit. The signal chart is posted where operators and signalers can see it, and the signals to be used are agreed before the lift, not discovered during it.Hanford states the exception precisely: if compliance with these hand signals is impractical for the job being performed, other hand signals shall be agreed on by the operator and signal person, and radio may be substituted by the same agreement. Non-standard signals are permitted — invented ones are not. The difference is the agreement, made in advance.
  9. Which rulebook you are actually under. This module has the opposite branch profile to OHC-05. On the facility branch — permanently installed, §1926.1438(a) — only §1910.179 applies, and §1910.179 says almost nothing about signals: a warning device at (i), and a warning signal at (n)(3)(xi). No signal chart, no signal person qualification, no voice protocol.On the construction branch the enumeration at (b)(2)(i) pulls in §§1926.1419 through 1422 and §1926.1428. Those paragraphs are now held from OSHA.gov and are gated below. EM 385 §16.G.05 remains the federal-work overlay (2014 numbering until the 2024 Chapter 16 body is re-read).
  10. Construction-branch hand signals, from held paragraphs. §1926.1419(c)(1): when using hand signals, the Standard Method must be used. Exception: where that method is infeasible, or an operation is not covered, non-standard signals may be used under (c)(2).(c)(2): the signal person, operator, and lift director (where there is one) must contact each other prior to the operation and agree on the non-standard signals. §1926.1422: hand signal charts must be either posted on the equipment or conspicuously posted in the vicinity of the hoisting operations.
  11. One signaler, anyone may stop, operator's perspective. §1926.1419(h): only one person may give signals at a time, except (j). (j): anyone who becomes aware of a safety problem must alert the operator or signal person by giving the stop or emergency stop signal.(k): all directions given to the operator by the signal person must be given from the operator's direction perspective. (f): if the ability to transmit signals is interrupted, the operator must safely stop until it is reestablished and a proper signal is given and understood.
  12. Who may signal on the construction branch. §1926.1428(a): the employer of the signal person must ensure each signal person meets the Qualification Requirements in (c) prior to giving any signals, by third-party or employer qualified evaluator.(a)(2): an employer's assessment is not portable. (a)(3): documentation must specify each type of signaling. (c)(5): oral or written test, and a practical test.

Task B — Verbal and Radio Protocols

Conduct lifts on verbal direction with closed-loop communication.

  1. Closed loop, four steps. Verbal direction is not a stream of instructions. It is a loop that closes each time: direction given, readback by the receiver, execution, confirmation that it happened.The readback is the step people drop, and it is the step that catches the error. Hearing “down two feet” and saying “down two feet” costs a second and catches the difference between two feet and two inches.
  2. Radio discipline. A radio channel is shared infrastructure. Discipline means an assigned channel, identification on each transmission so everyone knows who is speaking to whom, and for blind work a continuous-communication rule rather than intermittent check-ins.§1926.1420 (construction branch, held): devices must be tested on site before operations; transmission must be through a dedicated channel (exception: multiple cranes coordinating); the operator's reception must be hands-free. EM 385 §16.G.05.a(2) is what puts federal work on radio beyond 100 ft or without line of sight.
  3. Anchor directions to the building, not to your body. “Left” and “right” depend on which way the speaker is facing. Two people facing each other have opposite lefts, and the load moves according to whichever one the operator guessed.Standardised vocabulary ties motion to fixed references: north, the press end, column line 4, toward the roll-up door. The reference is chosen in the pre-lift briefing and it does not move when people turn around.
  4. Communication lost means stop. §16.G.05.c is the rule and it is unambiguous: during operations requiring signals, the ability to transmit signals shall be maintained. If that ability is interrupted at any time, the operator shall safely stop operations requiring signals until it is re-established and a proper signal is given and understood.Three conditions to resume, not one: re-established, proper signal given, and understood. A radio crackling back to life is not permission to move.
  5. Silence is not consent. The dangerous reading of a dropout is that no instruction means keep going. On a blind placement the operator has no other information, and the load continues into a space nobody is watching.This is why the stop convention is agreed before the lift. Mid-motion is not when to negotiate what silence means.
  6. Mixed-language crews. Where the crew does not share a first language, the requirement is not goodwill; it is terminology alignment before the lift. Agree the motion words, agree who interprets, and confirm both directions of the loop work.The signal set is an asset here — hand signals are language-independent, and a crew that agrees on the Standard Method has a channel that does not depend on vocabulary at all.

Task C — Crew Coordination

Integrate spotters, riggers, and adjacent trades into the lift communication plan.

  1. The pre-lift briefing has six headings. Load — what it is and what it weighs. Path — where it goes and over what. Roles — who operates, who signals, who spots, who rigs. Signals — which set, which channel, which fixed references.Exclusion zones — where nobody stands. And stop-work authority — stated out loud, so that using it later does not require courage. A briefing that omits the last two has covered the mechanics and skipped the protection.
  2. Spotter is not signaler. The signaler directs the crane. The spotter watches something the operator and signaler cannot — path clearance, an adjacent hazard, a pinch point, traffic. Different job, different attention, and normally a different person.EM 385 does describe a signal person acting as a spotter for a specific watch, so the roles can combine by design. What causes incidents is combining them by accident — a spotter who starts giving motion signals, or a signaler who looks away to check clearance.
  3. Stop-work authority is universal or it is nothing. §16.G.05.d already grants it in the narrow case: an emergency stop may be given by anyone and must be obeyed. A functioning site extends that to any hazard, from any person, at any time.The hard part is not the authority; it is the without penalty. Authority that costs the user something is authority nobody uses twice. It has to be honoured visibly — thank the caller, check the concern, then resume.
  4. Production environments have other operators in them. In a live bay the crane shares space with machine operators, line personnel, forklifts and foot traffic, none of whom are part of the lift and most of whom are concentrating on something else.Coordination means telling them before you start, not warning them while you move. A lift path across a production line is a two-supervisor conversation before it is a crane movement.
  5. Crane-to-crane contact is an adjacent-crew event. EM 385-1-1 16-8.aa(6): contact with runway stops or other cranes is made with extreme caution, with particular care for persons on or below the crane, and only after making certain that persons on the other cranes are aware.Read that last clause as a communication requirement, because that is what it is. Before contact, the other crane's crew is told and has acknowledged. Not signalled at — aware. Cross-refs `OHC.08.B.K2`.
  6. Trades who arrive without knowing. The most common uninvited hazard is a competent person doing their own job who has no idea a lift is underway. They did not ignore the exclusion zone; they never knew it existed.Which makes the control physical and informational rather than verbal: barriers and signage that work on someone who was never at the briefing, plus a spotter watching the approaches rather than the load.
