What they should be able to do after the presentation

  • Respond to loss of power or control with a suspended load, secure the area, and hand the recovery to the people authorised to perform it.
  • Recognise overtravel, brake and mechanical malfunctions as stop triggers, and hold the crane out of service until it has been inspected.
  • Manage dropped loads, contact events, injuries and fire, preserve the scene, and produce a factual account.

Talk track — present this, then gate

Task A — Power and Control Failures

Respond to loss of power or control with a suspended load.

  1. Power loss holds the load — by design. §1910.179(a)(20) defines it: a holding brake is a brake that automatically prevents motion when power is off. And §1910.179(f)(2)(iii) requires it: holding brakes on hoists shall be applied automatically when power is removed.So the expected behaviour on a power failure is that the load stops and stays. The hazard of a power loss is not the drop — it is everything people do next.
  2. Two brake systems, and a branch split. §1910.179(f)(1)(i): each independent hoisting unit shall have at least one self-setting brake, the holding brake, applied directly to the motor shaft or part of the gear train. (f)(1)(ii) adds a second system — control braking means to prevent overspeeding, in addition to the holding brake.Note the branch line. (f)(1) is in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list, so the self-setting brake is required on both branches. (f)(2) is not — so the *applied automatically when power is removed* wording, and the torque percentages at (f)(2)(i), are facility branch. The ACS's phrase “spring-set” names a common mechanism; the standard names a behaviour, and is deliberately mechanism-agnostic.
  3. A stranded load is a scene, not a problem to solve. The load is held. That is the system working. The correct response is to secure the area beneath and around it, keep everyone out, and notify. Nothing about a stranded load requires speed.What it does not require is improvisation. Recovery of a suspended load on a failed crane is an engineered operation, and the operator's job ends at securing and reporting.
  4. Never release a brake to get a load down. The single most dangerous action available to a person standing next to a stranded load is manually releasing the holding brake. There is no controlled descent at the other end of that decision — there is a free fall with the load's full weight and no braking system in the path.§1910.179(l)(3)(i) closes the door on it from the other side: adjustments and repairs shall be done only by designated personnel. Releasing a brake is an adjustment.
  5. The disconnect, and when to use it. §1910.179(g)(5)(i) puts the runway conductor supply on a switch or breaker on a fixed structure, accessible from the floor, lockable in the open position. (g)(5)(ii) and (iii) put a second one on the crane, reachable by the operator.Isolate when the crane is doing something it was not told to do, or when it must stay stopped while people work near or under it. §1910.179(l)(2)(i)(c) is the standard's own sequence: the main or emergency switch open and locked in the open position. That lockout implements the employer's §1910.147 energy-control programme (held). The operator is typically an affected employee — isolation is applied by an authorized employee.
  6. What a control malfunction looks like. Three signatures. Unresponsive — a motion that will not start. Sticking — a controller or button that does not return to off when released. Self-motion — the crane moving with no input at all.The standard treats the second and third as regulated failures. §1910.179(g)(3)(vi) requires floor-operated controllers to automatically return to the off position when released; (g)(3)(vii) requires pendant pushbuttons to return to off when pressure is released; (g)(3)(ix) requires that on remote-operated cranes, if the control signal becomes ineffective the crane motion shall stop. A control that holds a motion after release is a failed safety function, not a quirk.
  7. Why the E-stop is the answer and the controller is not. §1910.179(a)(59): an emergency stop switch is a switch to cut off electric power independently of the regular operating controls.That phrase is the whole reason it exists. If the controller is the thing that has failed, then working the controller harder is asking the failed component to fix itself. The E-stop takes a different path to the same result. On a sticking or self-moving crane it is the first action, not the last resort. §1910.179(a)(40) names the design principle: fail-safe means a provision designed to automatically stop or safely control any motion in which a malfunction occurs.
  8. Re-energising is a decision, and it is not the operator's. Restoring power to a crane whose failure is not understood can restart the motion that caused the problem — now with people nearby who believe the crane is dead.The failure is diagnosed before the crane is re-energised, by the people authorised to diagnose it. The operator's contribution is an accurate account of what the crane did, which is worth more than a theory about why.

Task B — Overtravel and Mechanical Malfunctions

Respond to limit failures, brake malfunction, and abnormal mechanical events.

  1. A two-block event is a rope event. Two-blocking loads the rope and reeving against a hard stop. The damage is in the rope, the sheaves and the terminations, and it is frequently invisible from the floor.The response is stop, and do not move — not even to lower the load, until the rope and reeving have been assessed. The next motion is the one that finds out whether the rope is still whole.
  2. A limit switch that fails verification. §1910.179(n)(4)(i) ends with the duty: if the switch does not operate properly, the appointed person shall be immediately notified. Immediately, and to a named role.The ACS states this as “crane out of service.” That is the right outcome, and it arrives via §1910.179(l)(3)(i) — unsafe conditions disclosed by inspection corrected before operation is resumed. The regulation names the notification; the out-of-service state follows from it. Teach both halves, because the notification is the one that gets skipped.
  3. Brake drift is not a control problem. A load that creeps down with the controller at off is a brake that is no longer holding. The instinct is to correct it with hoist inputs — and that hides a failing brake behind operator effort for as long as the operator keeps doing it.The correct response is to set the load down at the first safe opportunity and take the crane out of service. Remember there are two systems: the holding brake at (f)(1)(i) and the control braking means at (f)(1)(ii). Drift points at the holding brake.
  4. Noise, vibration and rope behaviour are stop triggers. A new sound, a new vibration, or rope that is doing something it did not do yesterday — jumping a groove, spooling unevenly, slapping — is information arriving early. Machines usually announce failures before they complete them.The discipline is treating new as the trigger, not loud. A quiet new noise is a better signal than a loud familiar one.
  5. Equipment character is a story people tell. Every long-serving crane accumulates a set of noises the crew has agreed to find normal. Some genuinely are. The problem is that the agreement is never revisited, so a new sound gets absorbed into the existing story on the day it appears.The counter is a written baseline and a low threshold for reporting. “It's always done that” is a claim about history, and it needs a date.
  6. Post-event inspection before return to service. After any two-block, contact or mechanical event, the crane is inspected before it runs again. §1910.179(l)(3)(i) requires unsafe conditions corrected before operation is resumed, and (l)(2)(ii) holds the crane until all guards are reinstalled, safety devices reactivated, and maintenance equipment removed.Both are in paragraphs (l) — facility branch. On the construction branch the inspection authority is §1926.1412 (held in OHC-05): a competent person begins the shift visual inspection under (d)(1). The operator's duty is the same either way: the crane does not run until somebody qualified says it may.

Task C — Emergency Events

Manage dropped loads, contact events, injuries, and fire.

  1. Dropped load and struck-by, in order. The sequence is fixed and the order matters: aid to anyone injured, control of the area so nobody becomes the second casualty, then notification up the line.Aid comes first, but it does not come recklessly. A dropped load leaves an unstable scene — the load itself, the rigging, whatever it struck, and possibly the crane. Entering an uncontrolled scene to help is how one casualty becomes two.
  2. Contact events hold two machines, not one. A crane-to-crane or crane-to-structure contact puts both systems in question. The other crane took the same impact, and the structure may have taken load it was not designed for.So the hold is on both. The instinct is to check your own crane, find nothing, and carry on — leaving a struck crane in service with an undiscovered defect and another operator on it. Cross-refs `OHC.08.B.R3`.
  3. Fire: the cab is a dead end. §1910.179(o)(3) sets the baseline: the employer shall ensure operators are familiar with the operation and care of the fire extinguishers provided. Familiar with, before the day it matters.The extinguisher decision is bounded: fight only a small fire, only with your egress secured behind you, and only if it is not growing. A cab has one way out along a walkway and down a ladder. If the fire is between you and that route, the decision is already made.
  4. The extinguisher prohibition still in the standard. §1910.179(c)(3) is two words long in substance: carbon tetrachloride extinguishers shall not be used.It reads as a historical artefact and it is live regulatory text. Carbon tetrachloride is acutely toxic and decomposes in heat to phosgene — in a cab, the extinguisher would be more dangerous than the fire. Both (c) and (o) are facility branch; neither is in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list.
  5. Electrical fire is an isolation problem first. A crane fire is usually electrical, and an energised electrical fire cannot be put out in any durable way while it is still fed. The first useful action is removing the supply — the runway disconnect at §1910.179(g)(5)(i), lockable and accessible from the floor.Never water on an energised fire. And a de-energised electrical fire is still hot and still full of stored energy in capacitors and windings.
  6. Preserve the scene. The instinct after an incident is to tidy up — move the load, coil the slings, get the crane out of the way. Every one of those destroys the record of what happened.Nothing moves until the scene has been documented and released, except what must move to reach an injured person or to remove an active hazard. That exception is narrow and it is the only one.
  7. Your account, and what makes it useful. The operator writes what they saw, heard and did, with times, in the order it happened. That is the highest-value document in any investigation, because it is the only record of the inside of the cab.Keep it factual and keep speculation out of it — not to protect anyone, but because a guess written down becomes a finding that has to be disproved later. “The hoist did not stop when I released the button” is worth more than “I think the contactor stuck.”

Teach it correctly — do not guess

  • ⭐ The E-stop has a regulatory definition, and it is the reason the E-stop is the answer. §1910.179(a)(59): an emergency stop switch is a switch to cut off electric power independently of the regular operating controls. That clause is the whole argument for A.K4b. If the controller is the component that failed, working it harder asks the failure to repair itself; the E-stop reaches the same result by a different path. Pair it with (a)(40), which defines fail-safe as a provision designed to automatically stop or safely control any motion in which a malfunction occurs. The ACS asserts immediate E-stop response without either citation.
  • ✅ Three control failures the standard already regulates. A.K4 asks for unresponsive, sticking, or self-motion conditions. Two of the three are named safety functions: (g)(3)(vi) floor-operated controllers shall automatically return to the off position when released; (g)(3)(vii) pendant pushbuttons shall return to off when pressure is released; and (g)(3)(ix) on remote-operated cranes, if the control signal becomes ineffective the crane motion shall stop. A motion that continues after release is a failed safety function, not equipment character. (g) is in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list — both branches.
  • ✅ A.K1b — brakes split across the branch line, and the ACS names a mechanism where the standard names a behaviour. §1910.179(f)(1)(i) requires at least one self-setting brake per independent hoisting unit, and (f)(1) is in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list — both branches. (f)(2) is not, so applied automatically when power is removed at (f)(2)(iii) and the torque percentages at (f)(2)(i) (125% / 100% / 100% each) are facility branch. Separately, the ACS says the load is held by spring-set brakes. Spring-set is the common mechanism; the standard specifies self-setting and automatic on power removal — a behaviour, deliberately mechanism-agnostic. Practically identical, but a learner who is taught spring will not recognise a compliant brake that works another way.
  • ✅ B.K2 — the regulation names a notification, not an out-of-service state. The ACS states B.K2 as “crane out of service.” That is the right outcome, but the regulation gets there in two steps. §1910.179(n)(4)(i) ends: if the switch does not operate properly, the appointed person shall be immediately notified. The out-of-service state then follows from §1910.179(l)(3)(i) — unsafe conditions corrected before operation is resumed. B.K2 gates the notification and B.K2b gates the source of the hold, because the notification is the half that gets skipped. Note the branch difference too: (n) binds on both branches, (l) is facility only.
  • ✅ Two fire provisions, one of them startling. §1910.179(o)(3): the employer shall ensure operators are familiar with the operation and care of the fire extinguishers provided — C.K3. And §1910.179(c)(3), which is live regulatory text and reads like an artefact: carbon tetrachloride extinguishers shall not be used — C.K3b. Carbon tetrachloride is acutely toxic and decomposes in heat to phosgene, so in a cab the extinguisher would be worse than the fire. Both (c) and (o) are facility branch; neither is in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list.
  • ⓘ Elements carrying a second item. A.K1, A.K3, A.K4, B.K2 and C.K3 each carry two items. A.K3b maps held §1910.147 onto mainline disconnect use: the operator is typically an affected employee.
