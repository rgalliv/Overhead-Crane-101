Task A

Personnel Protection Rules

Enforce the exclusion and protection rules around suspended loads.

Task A

The rule, and what the rule actually says

§1910.179(n)(3)(vi): the employer shall require that the operator avoid carrying loads over people. Note the shape of it — it is written as an employer duty to require avoidance, not as an absolute prohibition on the operator.

Nearly every facility programme converts it into an absolute: no load over personnel, ever. That is a defensible tightening and it is the rule you will be held to. Know both — the regulation's wording, and your programme's.
Task A

Path planning is the control

“Avoid” is not a thing you do at the controls. It is a thing you do before you pick, by choosing a route that does not cross occupied ground. Once the load is up and travelling, your options have already narrowed.

The path is planned on the floor plan, not on the fly: where are people working, where are the walkways, where are the doors people come through without looking up.
Task A

Nobody rides anything

§1910.179(n)(3)(v): while any employee is on the load or hook, there shall be no hoisting, lowering, or traveling. Not slowly, not briefly, not to reposition someone.

The prohibition extends by facility rule to riding the crane itself — the bridge, the walkway, the trolley — while it is in motion. An overhead crane is not transport.
Task A

The warning device, and its one exception

EM 385-1-1 16-8.aa(4): an alarm or other effective warning signal is required for each crane with a power traveling mechanism — except for floor-operated cranes.

§1910.179(n)(3)(xi) then names when it is used: when starting the bridge, and when the load or hook approaches near or over personnel. Two triggers, not one.
Task A

A warning is not a clearance

This is the limit the ACS asks you to understand. Sounding the alarm announces the crane. It does not authorise the movement, it does not clear the path, and it does not transfer responsibility to the people who heard it.

Using the horn as a substitute for looking is the failure mode. If the warning is doing the work of the exclusion zone, there is no exclusion zone — there is a noise and a hope that everyone interprets it the same way you do.
Task A

Attention is a control, and it is spendable

§1910.179(n)(3)(x): the employer shall ensure the operator does not leave the position at the controls while the load is suspended. Physically present is the floor of the requirement, not the ceiling of it.

A suspended load is an active hazard for as long as it hangs. The standard also polices the station itself — (o)(2) requires clothing and belongings stored so as not to interfere with access or operation, and tools kept in the tool box rather than loose in or about the cab.
Task A

The fall zone drifts inward

On a long operation the exclusion zone quietly shrinks. People who were clear at the start step closer to see, to help, to talk. Nobody decides to enter the fall zone; they arrive in it by degrees.

This is why the rule is stop motion, not shout. Re-establishing the zone costs seconds. Discovering it has closed costs somebody.

Task B

Multi-Crane and Shared-Runway Operations

Operate safely where cranes share runways, bays, or airspace at different elevations.

Task B

Three clearance relationships, continuously

EM 385-1-1 16-8.aa(5): clearance shall be maintained between the crane and any structure or object, any parallel running cranes, and cranes operating at different elevations.

§1910.179 backs two of the three: (b)(6)(i) sets the installed clearance from the building, and (b)(7) requires clearance between parallel cranes. Both apply on either branch. The elevation case is EM 385's contribution.
Task B

Different elevations is the one that surprises people

A crane on another runway at another height is outside the normal scan. It is not in your bay, not at your eye level, and its load block may occupy airspace you think of as empty.

Runway heights differ by design in older buildings and in bays that were extended. Establish the geometry once, deliberately, rather than discovering it with a load in the air.
Task B

Contact is permitted, but it is never casual

EM 385-1-1 16-8.aa(6): contacts with runway stops or other cranes shall be made with extreme caution, with particular care for persons on or below the crane, and only after making certain that persons on the other cranes are aware.

It does not prohibit contact. Reaching a runway stop is a normal end-of-travel event. What it requires is that contact be slow, intended, and known about by everyone it could affect — including anyone on the other crane.
Task B

Right of way is negotiated, not assumed

Two operators who each believe the other will yield are two operators on a collision course. Shared-bay movement runs on explicit communication: who is moving, where, and when the other is clear.

For a genuinely shared lift, §1910.179(n)(3)(ix) makes it formal — when two or more cranes lift one load, one qualified responsible person shall be in charge, and shall analyse the operation and instruct everyone on positioning, rigging and the movements to be made.
Task B

Parking and priority are facility conventions

Where cranes share a runway, the facility sets where each parks, who has priority through a pinch point, and which end of the bay is whose. These are local rules and they are not in any standard.

That does not make them optional. An operator who does not know the local parking convention will eventually park a crane where the next one needs to be, and the resolution happens under time pressure.
Task B

Interlocks and zone limiting

Where fitted, interlocks and zone-limiting systems prevent two cranes occupying the same space, or keep a crane out of a defined area. They are engineered controls and they are more reliable than attention.

They also breed dependence. Know whether your bay has them, what they cover, and — critically — what they do not cover. A zone limiter that protects one pinch point does not protect the other three.
Task B

The low-tech interlock: rail stops

§1910.179(l)(2)(i) closes its lockout sequence with the step that protects an idle crane: where other cranes are on the same runway, rail stops or other suitable means shall be provided to prevent interference with the idle crane.

This is the provision that keeps a working crane from running into one that is under maintenance with a mechanic on it. It is facility branch — (l) is not in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list.

Task C

Securing and Shutdown

Secure the crane correctly at task end, shift end, and adverse conditions.

Task C

The end-of-use sequence

Securing is a sequence, and each step answers a specific question. Hook empty — nothing is left hanging. Load block raised clear — clear of traffic, walkways and work at floor level. Controls off — every controller returned to the off position.

Then disconnect as required by the facility: main switch, disconnect, or lockout where the programme calls for it. A crane left live with the block at head height is secured in name only.
Task C

Outdoor cranes: secure them when you leave

EM 385-1-1 16-8.aa(7) is one sentence and it is absolute: operators of outdoor cranes shall secure them when leaving. Not at shift end — when leaving.

Wind does not wait for the end of the shift, and an unsecured outdoor crane is a wheeled structure on a rail with nothing holding it.
Task C

Wind indicators and rail clamps — read the scope

§1910.179(b)(4) requires that outdoor storage bridges be provided with automatic rail clamps, and that a wind-indicating device give a visible or audible alarm to the bridge operator at a predetermined wind velocity.

Two precisions. It is scoped to outdoor storage bridges, not to every outdoor crane. And (b)(4) is not in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list — the construction branch gets (b)(5), (6) and (7) only — so this is a facility-branch requirement. Elsewhere, the manufacturer's storm provisions and the facility programme govern.
Task C

Pendant and transmitter

A pendant left hanging within reach, or a radio transmitter left switched on and unattended, is an unsecured crane regardless of what the main switch is doing. The control is the access point.

Transmitters are stored and, where the system supports it, keyed off or de-energised. Pendants are returned to their park position. The question to answer is: could someone who is not qualified move this crane right now?
Task C

What actually permits leaving the station

§1910.179(n)(3)(x) forbids leaving the controls while the load is suspended. So the first condition is unconditional: the load is landed.

Then the rest: hook empty, block clear, controls off, and the crane secured to whatever level the facility requires for the duration you will be away. A break is not a special case with a lower bar.
Task C

Loads are not left hanging over breaks

This is the one that gets rationalised. The load is nearly placed, the break is short, moving it costs five minutes. So it hangs — unattended, over ground nobody is now watching, with no operator at the controls.

Every element of the rule is broken at once: (n)(3)(x) attendance, the exclusion zone, and the securing sequence. Land the load.
Task C

Handing over the crane

The incoming operator inherits the equipment and needs its actual state: what was run, what was noticed, what is outstanding, and whether anything is restricted or tagged.

A handoff that says “it's fine” transfers the crane without transferring the information. Where a defect exists, OHC-05 governs — tag, log, and say it out loud.
