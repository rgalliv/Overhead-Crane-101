Task A — Structural Systems

Identify structural components and their inspection significance.

  1. The load path. Every pound in the hook travels the same road: hook and load block, rope or chain, drum, trolley frame, trolley wheels, bridge girder, end trucks, bridge wheels, runway rail, runway beam, and finally the building columns and foundations.Naming the path in order is not an academic exercise. It is how you work out what a noise, a vibration or a crack actually threatens.
  2. Runways and anchorages. Runway beams, rails, rail joints and their alignment carry everything the crane carries. Foundations, anchorages, runways and rail tracks are constructed or installed per the manufacturer's recommendations and ANSI/ASME B30.2 or B30.17 as applicable.That requirement is explicit in the federal supplemental rules for overhead and gantry cranes, and it applies whether or not the crane is permanently installed.
  3. Stops, bumpers and sweeps. Trolley stops are provided at the limits of travel and are fastened to resist the forces applied when contacted. A stop engaging the tread of the wheel is at least equal to the radius of the wheel.Bumpers absorb energy; stops arrest travel; rail sweeps ride ahead of the truck wheels and extend below the top of the rail, pushing debris off the rail before a wheel can climb it.
  4. Deceleration criteria. Bumper performance is specified, not approximate. Bridge bumpers stop the crane at an average deceleration not exceeding 3 ft/s² at 20% of rated load speed, with energy capacity to stop at 40%. Trolley bumpers do not exceed 4.7 ft/s² at one-third rated load speed.Bumpers are also mounted so there is no direct shear on bolts, and designed to minimise parts falling from the crane if they break. Where two trolleys share a bridge, each is bumpered on its adjacent end.
  5. Walkways and access. Footwalks, service platforms, ladders and stairways are structure too. Footwalks are rigid, carry at least 50 lb/ft² distributed, have an antislip surface, and never provide less than 48 inches of headroom.Fixed ladders, toeboards and handrails must comply with Subpart D of Part 1910 — the crane standard hands off to it by name three separate times.
  6. Reading structural distress. Camber is designed-in upward curvature; deflection is what the load takes back. Loss of camber, growing deflection, cracked or corroded members, loose bolts or rivets and distorted connections are the periodic inspection's structural list.An operator is not the inspector, but is the only person who sees the crane every shift. Report location-specifically: which girder, which end, which joint — not "it looks bad."

Task B — Mechanical Systems

Identify hoist, trolley and drive components and their operating behaviour.

  1. The hoisting train. Drum, rope reeving, sheaves, load block and hook assembly with its safety latch make up the hoisting train. Sheave grooves are smooth and free from surface defects that could damage rope, and all running sheaves have a means of lubrication.Bottom-block sheaves carry close-fitting guards so the rope cannot foul when the block is on the ground with the ropes slack. That guard exists because of a specific, repeated failure.
  2. Rope and reeving. The relationship is fixed by rule: the rated load divided by the number of parts of rope shall not exceed 20% of the nominal breaking strength of the rope — a 5:1 design factor.More parts of line means more mechanical advantage and proportionally slower hook speed. Four-part reeving gives 4:1 at the hook and one quarter of the drum line speed.
  3. Two braking systems. Each independent hoisting unit has at least one self-setting holding brake applied to the motor shaft or gear train. It engages automatically whenever power is removed — it is the fail-safe.Each unit also has a control braking means to prevent overspeeding, with one exception: worm-geared hoists whose worm angle already prevents the load accelerating in the lowering direction. That exception is why two systems exist.
  4. Chain as a hoisting medium. Load chain and chain hoists are an alternate hoisting medium with their own consensus volume and their own rejection criteria — wear, twist, distorted links interfering with function, and stretch beyond the manufacturer's figures.Chain pockets and sheave flanges are dimensioned so the chain does not catch or bind during operation. Grade matters: only alloy chain rated for overhead lifting belongs on a hoist.
  5. The upper limit switch. The upper limit switch stops hoisting before the block reaches the trolley. Its trip setting is determined by test, with an empty hook, at increasing speeds up to maximum.That is the whole argument in one sentence: it is set by test, so it is a test device. The rule states it plainly — the hoist limit switch controlling upper travel of the load block shall never be used as an operating control.
  6. Drive train and duty class. Gear cases, couplings, wheels and the drive train move the crane. Wheels are removed from service at a 1/8 inch tread flat spot — usually detected first as a thumping during bridge travel.Duty classification rates a crane to its service severity. Class A is standby or infrequent service; Class F is continuous severe service — mills and hot metal. Duty class is on the data plate and it governs what the crane may be asked to do.

Task C — Electrical and Control Systems

Identify power delivery and control components and safe electrical interfaces.

  1. Getting power to a moving crane. Power reaches a travelling crane one of two ways: conductor bars with sliding collectors, or a festoon cable system running on a track.Festoon encloses its conductors. Conductor bar has exposed or semi-enclosed rails carrying lethal voltage along the whole runway. Both require lockout for any work on the crane; only one of them looks dangerous.
  2. The disconnect. The mainline disconnect is the operator's isolation point. Before adjustments or repairs the main or emergency switch is open and locked in the open position, all controllers are off, and warning or out-of-order signs go on the crane and on the floor beneath or the hook where they are visible from the floor.Where other cranes share the runway, rail stops or equivalent prevent them interfering with the idle crane. That is the step people skip.
  3. Control circuits. Wiring and equipment comply with Subpart S of Part 1910. Control circuit voltage does not exceed 600 V a.c. or d.c., and voltage at pendant push-buttons does not exceed 150 V a.c. or 300 V d.c.Those pendant limits exist because the pendant is the one energised thing an operator holds in wet hands all shift.
  4. The warning device. Except for floor-operated cranes, an alarm or other effective warning signal is provided for each crane equipped with a power travelling mechanism.Note the exception and note the branch. On the facility branch this is a §1910.179 requirement; it is not among the paragraphs carried onto the non-permanently-installed construction branch. On federal work it applies either way.
  5. Operator control hardware. Pendants need working strain relief so the cable never carries the pendant's weight. Enclosures must be intact, push-button covers unsplit, legends legible, and lever-operated controls must spring-return to neutral.Wireless transmitters add battery state, pairing and loss-of-signal behaviour to the same list. A transmitter with an unknown battery is an unknown crane.
  6. When the electrics misbehave. Intermittent power pickup — a dirty collector, a worn shoe, a damaged festoon trolley — produces motion you did not command or loses motion you did.There is one correct response and it does not involve diagnosis: bring the load down under control if you can, stop, secure the crane, and report it. Operators do not troubleshoot energised crane electrics.

Teach it correctly — do not guess

  • ⚠️ CMAA 74 not held. OHC.02.B.K5 duty classification is authored from CMAA 70, which is in the corpus. The ACS reference block cites CMAA Spec 70 / 74; 74 is not held. Duty-class concepts are common to both, so the item stands — but any single-girder-specific claim needs 74 before it is made.
  • ⚠️ Branch-dependent content. OHC.02.C.K4 (warning device) rests on §1910.179(i), which is not among the paragraphs carried onto the §1926.1438(b) construction branch. EM 385 §16.M.04 restores it on federal work regardless of installation status. The item is written to the facility branch; the branch split itself is taught in OHC-01.
  • ⚠️ Also facility-branch only. OHC.02.A.K3 draws on §1910.179(e)(2) bridge bumpers and (e)(4) rail sweeps, neither of which is in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list. (e)(1) trolley stops and (e)(3) trolley bumpers do carry across.
  • ✅ Part 1910 handoffs honoured. OHC.02.A.K4 cites Subpart D and OHC.02.C.K3 cites Subpart S, the two handoffs §1910.179 makes by name. This closes the OHC-02 half of the Part 1910 map.
