What they should be able to do after the presentation

  • Perform and document the daily and frequent inspection scope for the assigned crane, including the correct limit-switch verification technique.

Talk track — present this, then gate

Task A — Pre-Operational and Frequent Inspection

Perform the daily and frequent inspection scope for the assigned crane.

  1. Two regimes, two clocks. §1910.179(j)(1)(ii) splits inspection into two intervals and names them. Frequent is daily to monthly. Periodic is one to twelve months. Everything in this module sits in one of those two boxes.The operator owns the frequent end. The periodic end belongs to a designated person — but it runs on what the operator reports.
  2. The daily scope. Four things are inspected daily under (j)(2): all functional operating mechanisms for maladjustment interfering with proper operation; deterioration or leakage in air or hydraulic systems; hooks for deformation or cracks; and hoist chains for wear, twisted links, distorted links and stretch.Hooks and chains carry a second obligation: a monthly inspection with a certification record. Daily is visual. Monthly is documented.
  3. The certification record has three fields. For hooks (j)(2)(iii), chains (j)(2)(iv) and ropes (m)(1), the record is not free-form. It must carry the date of the inspection, the signature of the person who performed it, and the serial number or other identifier of the item inspected.An inspection log with a date and a tick, and no identifier for the hook, does not satisfy the standard. The item has to be identifiable later.
  4. Function checks: every motion, every brake. Run each motion — hoist, trolley, bridge — in both directions. Prove each brake holds. Prove the warning device sounds. Prove the emergency stop stops. A crane that moves is not a crane that has been checked.§1910.179(n)(3)(vii) requires the brakes to be tested by lifting the load a few inches and applying the brakes whenever the load approaches rated capacity.
  5. The limit switch is a test device, not a control. §1910.179(n)(4)(i) requires the upper limit switch of each hoist to be tried out at the beginning of each shift, under no load. Take it up slowly, or inch it at slow speed into the limit.(n)(4)(ii) then says it plainly: the limit switch shall not be used as an operating control. Something that stops the hoist every shift by design is a device that is wearing out on purpose.
  6. Hooks: two figures, one rule for choosing. OSHA (j)(2)(iii) removes a hook at more than 15% in excess of normal throat opening, more than 10° twist from the plane of the unbent hook, or any crack. ASME B30.10 is stricter — 5%, not to exceed ¼ in, plus any visible bend or twist.The rule for choosing, from the DOE Hanford manual's Inconsistent Standards discussion: follow ASME on the criteria, follow OSHA on the records. ASME sets the tighter number; OSHA is what requires the monthly documented inspection.

Task B — Periodic Inspection Interface

Describe the periodic inspection programme and the operator's interface with it.

  1. Whose inspection it is. The periodic inspection is a designated person function. It is not something an operator performs, signs, or substitutes for. What the operator owes it is observations — the things noticed at the frequent interval that a quarterly walk-down would never see.§1910.179 also uses a second term the ACS does not: appointed person, at (j)(4), (k)(2), (m)(1), (m)(2) and (n)(4)(i). Designated operates. Appointed approves and receives records.
  2. What periodic actually covers. (j)(3) names the scope: deformed, cracked or corroded members; loose bolts or rivets; cracked or worn sheaves and drums; worn, cracked or distorted pins, bearings, shafts, gears, rollers, locking and clamping devices; excessive wear on brake parts, linings, pawls and ratchets.It also covers load, wind and other indicators over their full range, powerplants, chain drive sprockets and chain stretch, and electrical apparatus for pitting or deterioration of controller contactors, limit switches and pushbutton stations.
  3. Idle and standby cranes. (j)(4) sets three cases. A crane idle one to six months gets the frequent (j)(2) inspection plus the (m)(2) rope inspection before return to service. A crane idle over six months gets a complete (j)(2) and (j)(3) inspection, plus (m)(2).Standby cranes — not idle, just not in regular use — get (j)(2) and (m)(2) at least semi-annually.
  4. Rope has its own clock. (m)(1) requires a thorough inspection of all running ropes at least once a month, with a certification record kept on file where readily available to appointed personnel.(m)(2): a rope that has been idle a month or more gets a thorough inspection before use, by an appointed person whose approval is required.
  5. After a repair, before use. (l)(2)(ii) is the return-to-service rule and it is three conditions, all of them: the crane is not operated until all guards are reinstalled, safety devices are reactivated, and maintenance equipment is removed.A reactivated crane with a bypassed limit switch and a jumper still fitted is the classic post-maintenance incident. Two of the three conditions were met.
  6. The construction branch is a different authority. §1910.179(j), (l) and (m) are not in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list. On a crane that is not permanently installed and is used in construction, the inspection duty comes from §1926.1412 and wire rope from §1926.1413 (both held from OSHA.gov).§1926.1412(d)(1): a competent person must begin a visual inspection prior to each shift the equipment will be used. (e)(1) monthly repeats the shift scope. (f)(2) at least every 12 months, a qualified person. §1926.1413(a)(1): a competent person begins a visual inspection of wire ropes prior to each shift. Do not mix Category I/II/III rope removal into this module's C.K2 — that list stays on facility §1910.179(m).

Task C — Deficiency Response and Removal from Service

Remove defective equipment from service and control it against use.

  1. Stop-work is not a request. (l)(3)(i) is the operator's authority in one line: unsafe conditions disclosed by the (j) inspection shall be corrected before operation is resumed. Not logged for later, not worked around, not deferred to the next shift.The same paragraph closes the other half: adjustments and repairs shall be done only by designated personnel. The operator stops the crane. The operator does not fix the crane.
  2. Six ways a rope loses strength. (m) names the conditions: reduction of rope diameter below nominal, from loss of core support, internal or external corrosion, or wear of outside wires; the number of broken outside wires and the degree of distribution or concentration of them; worn outside wires.And three more that get forgotten: corroded or broken wires at end connections; corroded, cracked, bent, worn or improperly applied end connections; severe kinking, crushing, cutting or unstranding.
  3. Distribution, not just a count. Six broken wires spread over a long length of rope and six broken wires in one strand in one lay are not the same finding. The standard asks for the number and the degree of distribution or concentration.This is what separates reading a rope from counting one. Concentration is the signal — it says the damage has a cause and a location.
  4. Locking the crane out is five steps. (l)(2)(i), in order: run the crane to a location causing least interference with other cranes and operations; put all controllers in the off position; open and lock open the main or emergency switch; hang warning or out-of-order signs on the crane and on the floor beneath or on the hook where visible from the floor.Step five is the one that gets a mechanic hit: where other cranes share the runway, rail stops or other suitable means shall be provided to prevent interference with the idle crane. That crane-specific sequence is the isolate path. The energy-control programme behind lockout is §1910.147 (held). On the construction B30.2-2005 path, §1926.1438(b)(2)(iii) states that 29 CFR 1910.147 shall be substituted for ANSI Z244.1.
  5. Hooks are discarded, not repaired. (l)(3)(iii)(a): hooks with the (j)(2)(iii) defects shall be discarded. The standard's own words on the alternative — “repairs by welding or reshaping are not generally recommended.”If a repair is attempted at all, it is only under competent supervision, and the hook must be load tested per (k)(2) before it goes back into service.
  6. Handing over a defect. A defect that lives only in the finder's head is a defect the next shift will discover with a load on the hook. The tag on the crane, the entry in the log and the spoken handover are three channels and all three are cheap.Where the crane has been taken out of service, the incoming operator needs to know what is wrong, who was told, and what has to happen before it runs again.

Teach it correctly — do not guess

  • ⚠️ The hook criteria conflict, taught rather than hidden. OSHA §1910.179(j)(2)(iii) removes a hook at 15% throat opening and 10° twist. ASME B30.10 is stricter — 5%, not to exceed ¼ in, plus any visible bend or twist. Two Tier 0 rigging guides in the corpus teach one figure each and neither states the rule for choosing. The DOE Hanford manual (TR244C Rev 5) resolves it under a heading called Inconsistent Standards: follow ASME on the criteria, follow OSHA on the records. A.K4 and A.K4b teach both figures and the rule.
  • ✅ The intervals the ACS never states. OHC.05.A and OHC.05.B describe the frequent and periodic regimes without giving either interval. §1910.179(j)(1)(ii) has them: frequent is daily to monthly, periodic is one to twelve months. Both are now taught and gated.
  • ✅ The certification record is three named fields. The ACS says A.K5 is documentation per facility programme. The standard is more specific: for hooks, chains and ropes the record carries the date, the signature of the person who performed the inspection, and the serial number or other identifier of the item. A.K5 is written to the standard.
  • ✅ Idle and standby closed from concepts to numbers. B.K4 reads idle and standby crane inspection concepts in the ACS. §1910.179(j)(4) gives three cases: idle 1–6 months → (j)(2) + (m)(2); idle over 6 months → (j)(2) + (j)(3) + (m)(2); standby → (j)(2) + (m)(2) at least semi-annually.
  • ✅ Construction-branch inspection paragraphs are held. Cross-check finding F4: §1910.179 (j), (l) and (m) are not in the §1926.1438(b)(2) list. Fetched 2026-08-28 from OSHA.gov. B.R3 gates §1926.1412(d)(1): a competent person must begin a visual inspection prior to each shift. Monthly (e)(1) and annual (f)(2) and wire-rope §1926.1413(a)(1) are taught from the citation pack. Category I/II/III rope-removal lists stay out of C.K2, which remains on facility §1910.179(m). C.K3b gates the §1926.1438(b)(2)(iii) substitution of §1910.147 for ANSI Z244.1 on the construction B30.2-2005 path. ACS B.R3 remains returning a repaired crane without verification (already gated at B.K5); the construction shift-inspection overlay occupies the B.R3 second-fact slot because that is where the branch hole was taught.
  • ✅ Distribution, not just a wire count. C.K2 in the ACS lists broken wires, kinking, crushing, corrosion, stretch, and gauge wear. §1910.179(m) names six conditions and two of them concern end connections, which the ACS omits entirely. It also asks for the number of broken wires and the degree of distribution or concentration — the part that separates reading a rope from counting one.
