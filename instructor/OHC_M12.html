Task A — Integrated Scenario Performance

Execute complete lift assignments integrating all prior competencies.

  1. The full cycle, in order, every time. Six phases, and none of them is optional: jurisdiction confirmation (which branch, which rulebook), inspection, planning, communication, execution, securing. OHC-01 through OHC-08 each taught one piece; the capstone is the first time you run all six as one thing.The order matters because each phase produces something the next one needs. Jurisdiction tells you which inspection regime applies. Inspection tells you whether there is a crane to plan with. Planning tells the crew what to communicate about.
  2. Jurisdiction first, because it changes the rules. The single most common structural error in crane training is teaching one rulebook. The same overhead crane, doing the same lift, is governed differently depending on whether it is permanently installed (§1926.1438(a) — all of §1910.179) or not (§1926.1438(b) — an enumerated subset plus Subpart CC).You have now met both faces of this. Inspection intervals and maintenance rules are facility branch (OHC-05). The signals regime is construction branch (OHC-09). Load handling binds on both (OHC-07). Confirming the branch is the first phase because it silently selects everything after it.
  3. Scenario variation is the point, not the padding. Assessment runs across the control modes and load types on your assigned equipment — pendant, cab, radio; compact and awkward loads; open floor and congested placement. A single scenario tests one habit. Variation tests competence.This is also how mode-specific habit transfer gets caught, which is the failure OHC-03 warned about: the cab operator's spatial reference, applied from the floor, puts the load the wrong way.
  4. Faults are injected, and nobody will point at them. Scenarios carry embedded faults and hazards, and detection is expected without prompting. An evaluator who has to ask “did you notice anything about the hook?” has already recorded the answer.This is deliberately harder than a checklist, because the real failure mode is an operator who completes every checklist item and walks past the defect between them.
  5. What a critical error is. Performance is graded on tolerances, sequence discipline, and a zero critical-error requirement. Tolerances and sequence can carry minor deductions. A critical error cannot.A critical error is one that would have injured somebody or wrecked equipment if the scenario had been real — a load over a person, a defect operated through, a brake left unproven, a placement into live equipment. There is no partial credit on those, and that is the same standard the 100% knowledge gate applies.
  6. Consistency is the actual assessment. `A.K5` is the element that makes this a standard of care rather than an exam: the standard is demonstrated as consistent behaviour, not test-day behaviour. Performance is sustained across consecutive scenarios, which is how the difference shows up.Anybody can run one clean lift while being watched. The question the capstone asks is what you do on the fourth one, when the evaluator has stopped writing and the shortcut is available.
  7. Time pressure is the test condition, not an excuse. Sequence shortcuts under pressure are the most common capstone failure, and the reason is that pressure is always present in real work. A sequence that only survives an unhurried shift is not a sequence you have.The counter is that the sequence is short and fixed. Slack, load, prove the brake. Confirm balance. Sound the warning. There is no version of a working day that does not have room for it.

Task B — Documentation and Records

Produce and maintain the records that evidence competency and equipment status.

  1. Records are the evidentiary layer. `B.K4` is the framing element and it is worth stating plainly: the standard of care is a behaviour, but in any audit, investigation or dispute it is only visible through records. An inspection that happened and was not recorded is, evidentially, an inspection that did not happen.That is not a reason to write fiction. It is a reason to record what you actually did, at the time you did it.
  2. The record the standard actually specifies. For hooks (j)(2)(iii), chains (j)(2)(iv) and ropes (m)(1), the certification record is not free-form. It carries three named fields: the date of the inspection, the signature of the person who performed it, and the serial number or other identifier of the item inspected.That third field is the one facility logs most often omit, and it is the one that makes the record mean anything later. A dated tick with no item identifier proves somebody looked at something.
  3. Where the records live. §1910.179(m)(1) requires the rope certification record be kept on file where readily available to appointed personnel. §1910.179(k)(2) requires rated load test reports to be on file where readily available to appointed personnel.“Readily available” is a real requirement, not a formality. A record in a system nobody can reach on the day of an incident is not on file in any useful sense. Knowing where is part of the competency.
  4. Equipment status records. Three documents move a crane through a defect: the deficiency report that raises it, the removal-from-service tag that controls the equipment, and the return-to-service verification that releases it.The standard specifies the middle one in detail — §1910.179(l)(2)(i) puts signs on the crane and on the floor beneath or on the hook where visible from the floor. And it specifies the third: (l)(2)(ii), guards reinstalled, safety devices reactivated, maintenance equipment removed; (l)(3)(i), unsafe conditions corrected before operation resumes.
  5. Training and evaluation records. Your personal record package supports somebody else's decision: the employer's qualification determination. It needs to show what you were trained on, what you were evaluated on, by whom, when, and on what equipment.Scope is the part people under-record. “Overhead crane training” is not a scope. “Pendant and radio, 5-ton and 10-ton single-girder, Bay 3 and Bay 4” is.
  6. Retention and audit readiness. Retention periods come from the facility programme and, for some records, from the regulation's own filing requirements. Audit readiness is simpler than it sounds: could you produce the last twelve months of your own records today, without help?If the answer is no, the records exist but the evidentiary layer does not.
  7. Records written afterwards. A record completed at the end of the week for four earlier shifts is a reconstruction, and reconstructions are systematically wrong in a specific direction — they record what should have happened.This is also the failure mode that produces the worst outcome in an investigation, because a record that is provably retrospective damages the credibility of every other record beside it.

Task C — Designation Gate and Certification Routing

Complete the gate architecture appropriate to the operating branch.

  1. The Designation Gate, and its citation. §1910.179(b)(8): “Only designated personnel shall be permitted to operate a crane covered by this section.” That single sentence is the Designation Gate.Note what it does not say. It does not require a certificate, a card, or a third party. It requires that the employer designate — and the employer therefore remains the qualified person making that determination. The same term scopes a second role at (l)(3)(i): adjustments and repairs only by designated personnel.
  2. Why there has to be a second gate. §1910.179(b)(8) is not in the §1926.1438(b)(2) enumeration. On the construction branch it simply does not apply — so “designated by the employer” has no legal force there.That absence is the whole reason the architecture has two gates rather than one. It is not a programme preference; it is what the incorporation list does and does not carry.
  3. The Certification Gate, and what is verified about it. On the §1926.1438(b) construction branch, operator qualification comes from §1926.1427 (held from OSHA.gov). That section does apply: the enumeration at §1926.1438(b)(2)(i) brings in §§1926.1427 through 1434. §1926.1427(d) requires certification by an accredited crane operator testing organization or a qualifying government licence. OSHA does not name NCCCO.NCCCO's public overview confirms a CCO Overhead Crane Operator certification (written + practical; cab or pendant/remote). CraneQualified routes that programme path through CCOS. That routing is administrative, not a CFR citation. Exam-item counts are not OSHA requirements and are not gated here.
  4. Your designation has edges. A designation is not a licence to operate cranes. It covers named equipment, named control modes, and named environments. Everything outside those names is uncovered, and operating there is outside your designation even though it is the same building and the same skill.Scope drift is quiet: a second crane arrives, a radio unit replaces a pendant, a bay is added. Nobody revokes anything — the work simply moves past the paper.
  5. What brings you back for re-evaluation. Four triggers, and only one is a calendar: new equipment or a new control mode, an incident, an observed deficiency, and lapsed activity — a long enough gap that currency is in question.The one most often ignored is the incident trigger, because re-evaluation after an event reads as blame. It is not. An incident is evidence that the operating picture changed, and re-evaluation is how the picture gets re-established.
  6. What this programme gives you, and what it does not. `C.K5` is the most important element in the track. Completing this programme establishes structured preparation and evidences your standard of care. It does not confer qualification.Qualification is a determination, and it belongs to the controlling entity — the employer — who knows the equipment, the site and the work. It is not delegable to a training vendor, including this one. Anybody who tells you a course made you qualified has misdescribed both the course and the law.
  7. Canada routing at the Designation Gate. If the work is Canadian, do not close the file with a US Certification Gate story. Complete the employer written authorization using the Canada jurisdiction pack (CSA B167 + federal/provincial overlay) and the EN/FR designation certificate.A US certification card is not sole evidence of Canadian competency. Keep PES sheets, gate records, and the signed designation together — that is the audit file.
  8. The package the safety director keeps. Minimum record set: 100% module gates, signed PES for skills in scope, jurisdiction determination (US branch or Canada branch), designation certificate scoped to equipment and modes, site familiarization, requalification triggers.Print docs/OHC-buyer-audit-binder.md and tick the checklist into the operator file.
